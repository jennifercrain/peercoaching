Week Two Training

Coaching for Objective Assessments

Helping students prepare to show what they know

Coaching for OA Success

Students may come to peers because they are nervous about an upcoming Objective Assessment, struggled on a previous attempt, feel thrown off by assessment questions, or simply want help preparing.

Peer support does not focus on predicting what will be on the assessment or determining whether a student knows enough to pass. Instead, interns help students strengthen the process of preparing for and approaching an assessment.

This may include:

  • • Practicing test-taking strategies
  • • Building confidence with unfamiliar or scenario-based questions
  • • Using a whiteboard effectively
  • • Identifying patterns that contribute to second-guessing or rushing
  • • Practicing retrieval and application of course material
  • • Connecting students to approved assessment and proctoring resources

The goal is not to prepare students for the questions they will see. The goal is to help students use what they know regardless of how a question is presented.

Look for Opportunities to Practice

OA strategies are most useful when students actually practice using them. Avoid simply recommending a strategy and moving on.

Instead of:

“You should try using a whiteboard during your OA.”

Practice it:

Pull up a small section of course material and an interactive whiteboard, such as a Canva whiteboard. Help the student identify information they may want available quickly during an assessment and practice organizing or writing it from memory.

The student leaves having actually rehearsed the process instead of only hearing about it.

Other Opportunities for Practice

“I always get stuck between two answers.”

→ Practice process of elimination with an approved practice question.

“The questions are worded so weirdly.”

→ Practice slowing down, identifying keywords, noticing negatives or qualifiers, and rephrasing the question.

“I know it until the test starts, and then I forget everything.”

→ Explore retrieval strategies such as brain dumping and practice what that process could look like.

“Scenario questions throw me off.”

→ Practice identifying the underlying concept in an approved practice question and connecting it back to the course material.

Whenever possible, move from talking about a strategy to practicing the strategy.

Coaching Students Who Feel Anxious About OAs

Students may describe freezing, going blank, second-guessing themselves, feeling panicked, or struggling to focus during an assessment.

Your role is not to diagnose the student or determine whether they have “test anxiety.” Coach what the student is experiencing.

Start with Listening and Exploration

Instead of immediately giving advice, help the student identify what is happening.

Powerful questions might include:

“What do you notice happening when you start to feel stuck?”
“At what point in the assessment does that usually start?”
“What tends to go through your mind when you aren't sure about an answer?”
“What have you tried so far when that happens?”
“What feels different when you're practicing compared with when you're actually taking the assessment?”

Their answers may reveal a need related to preparation, pacing, question interpretation, confidence, recall, or another part of the testing process.

Keep the Focus on the Student

Because you are also a WGU student, you may have your own experiences with difficult assessments. Personal experience can sometimes help normalize a student's feelings, but it should not take over the conversation.

Avoid:

“I had horrible test anxiety in this course too. Here's everything I did...”

Instead:

“I've experienced that feeling of getting stuck on an assessment too. What do you notice helps you regain your focus when that happens?”

If you share an experience, keep it brief, relevant, and followed by a coaching question that returns the focus to the student.

The Preassessment Is a Tool — Not the OA

Students sometimes say:

“The Preassessment was nothing like the OA.”

That does not necessarily mean something went wrong.

The Preassessment and Objective Assessment are not intended to feel the same.

The Preassessment provides a sampling of course topics and gives students an opportunity to gauge their readiness. Questions may ask students to recall information or demonstrate basic understanding.

Objective Assessments frequently require students to take that knowledge further by applying it, often through scenarios or unfamiliar situations.

This means:

  • • A topic appearing on the Preassessment does not guarantee that exact topic will appear on the OA.
  • • The number of Preassessment questions about a topic does not predict how many OA questions will address it.
  • • Students should not expect Preassessment wording or questions to resemble the OA.
  • • The Preassessment does not change, while students may encounter different versions of an Objective Assessment.
  • • Preparing only from Preassessment questions can leave students unprepared to apply their knowledge in new ways.

Always encourage thorough engagement with and review of the course material.

When the Difference Feels Smaller

As students develop a deeper and more flexible understanding of the material, unfamiliar questions may begin to feel less unfamiliar.

The questions do not necessarily become more similar. Instead, the student becomes better able to recognize the underlying concept and apply what they know even when it is presented differently.

That flexibility is what we want to help students build.

Using the Preassessment During Coaching

Students may ask:

“Can we go over my Preassessment?”

Peer interns do not review a Preassessment as an instructor, tutor, or evaluator.

The Preassessment should only be used during a coaching session when it provides material for practicing a specific learning or test-taking strategy.

Appropriate Uses

  • ✓ Practice process of elimination
  • ✓ Identify keywords, qualifiers, or double negatives
  • ✓ Practice slowing down and rephrasing a complicated question
  • ✓ Practice reasoning through scenario-based questions
  • ✓ Use a question to demonstrate an OA strategy from the Strategy Toolbox
  • ✓ Use the Feynman Technique to help the student explain their thinking
  • ✓ Return to the course material so the student can verify their own reasoning

Not Appropriate

  • ✗ Going through every missed question
  • ✗ Explaining why an answer is correct or incorrect
  • ✗ Evaluating whether the student understands the content
  • ✗ Telling the student whether they are ready for the OA
  • ✗ Using Preassessment questions to quiz the student
  • ✗ Treating the Preassessment as a study guide for what will appear on the OA

Peer interns do not quiz students.

Using Feynman Without Becoming the Evaluator

The Feynman Technique can be especially useful when practicing with an approved question because it keeps the thinking with the student.

For example:

1

Explain

Ask the student to explain the underlying concept or situation in their own words.

2

Connect

Have the student use their explanation to determine which answer choice best aligns with their current understanding.

3

Verify

Return to the course material together and have the student locate information that supports, challenges, or clarifies their reasoning.

4

Reflect

Ask what they noticed about their approach and what they might do when they encounter a similar situation on their own.

The peer intern does not determine whether the student's answer is correct. The course material becomes the source of verification.

Practicing Under Test-Like Conditions

The Preassessment can also provide an opportunity for students to practice the experience of taking an assessment, not just the questions.

Students might experiment with:

  • • Taking the Preassessment in a quiet environment
  • • Limiting distractions
  • • Practicing pacing
  • • Using only tools that would be available during the OA
  • • Practicing how they will use their whiteboard
  • • Applying a test-taking strategy intentionally throughout the attempt

Students may take the Preassessment more than once, but repeated attempts should not become an exercise in memorizing familiar questions.

The purpose is to strengthen assessment readiness and strategy use—not to learn the Preassessment itself.

OA Strategies and Resources

The Strategy Toolbox contains dedicated OA/Test-Taking strategies, demonstrations, templates, and links to additional Academic Coaching Center resources.

Resources are available for topics such as:

  • • Assessment fear and preparation
  • • Preparing for a first Objective Assessment
  • • Test-taking and multiple-choice strategies
  • • Reducing assessment anxiety
  • • Whiteboard use
  • • Online proctoring
  • • Assessment policies and requirements

You do not need to memorize every resource or explain every strategy during one session.

Identify the student's challenge, select the most relevant strategy or resource, and use it with them.

OA Retakes and Success Plans

Students who have an unsuccessful OA attempt may come to peers while completing a retake or success plan.

Peer interns may provide coaching support, but retake requirements belong to the Course Instructor.

Peer Interns May:

  • ✓ Help students practice an approved study or assessment strategy
  • ✓ Support planning, organization, confidence, or accountability
  • ✓ Help students locate and use approved course resources
  • ✓ Coach students on how they are approaching their preparation

Peer Interns May Not:

  • ✗ Administer a retake or success plan
  • ✗ Add, remove, waive, or modify its requirements
  • ✗ Decide whether a requirement has been completed satisfactorily
  • ✗ Approve another OA attempt
  • ✗ Perform Course Instructor evaluative activities

A Note About “Teachbacks”

Do not document that you completed a “Teachback” with a student.

Teachbacks are Course Instructor activities and may represent specific instructional or evaluative requirements.

Document what you actually coached instead.

“Practiced the Feynman Technique using course material and supported the student in verifying their reasoning with approved resources.”

“Practiced process of elimination and question interpretation using approved practice material.”

The Course Instructor determines whether retake requirements have been satisfied.

Remember

Do not predict. Do not quiz. Do not evaluate.

Help the student:

Practice → Reflect → Verify → Apply

Effective OA coaching helps students become more confident in their ability to approach assessments independently—not more dependent on a peer to tell them whether their answers are right.
