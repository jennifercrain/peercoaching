Week Two Training

Coaching for Performance Assessments

Helping students plan, organize, and move forward with their work

Coaching Through a Performance Assessment

Students may come to peer interns because they feel overwhelmed by a Performance Assessment, do not know where to begin, are struggling with their writing process, or feel stuck somewhere along the way.

Peer interns support the process of completing a Performance Assessment, not the Performance Assessment itself.

The goal is not to help the student complete the task. The goal is to help the student develop a process they can use to complete their own work independently.

This may include:

Breaking a large project into manageable steps
Identifying a starting point
Using available templates and resources
Building a planning or brainstorming process
Developing strategies for self-review
Organizing time and workload
Preparing questions for a Course Instructor, Writing Coach, Librarian, or other appropriate support person

A Critical Boundary: We Do Not Evaluate Performance Assessments

Peer interns do not perform evaluative functions.

This means you cannot:

×

Review a student's Performance Assessment

×

Read through their work and provide feedback

×

Work on the Performance Assessment with the student

×

Tell the student what to write

×

Suggest ideas, examples, arguments, or content for their response

×

Explain what a rubric requirement or task prompt is asking for

×

Tell the student whether they have answered a prompt

×

Determine whether their response meets a rubric requirement

×

Tell the student whether something is correct, sufficient, or ready to submit

×

Predict whether the Performance Assessment will pass

These responsibilities belong to the student, Course Instructor, and Evaluation faculty as appropriate.

Peer support focuses on the student's learning and writing process—not judging, interpreting, correcting, or completing their work.

Start by Finding the Real Barrier

A student saying, “I need help with my PA,” does not yet tell you what kind of support they need. Use powerful questions to explore where they are in the process and what is getting in the way.

Questions might include:

  • • “Where are you in the process right now?”
  • • “What feels hardest about moving forward?”
  • • “What have you tried so far?”
  • • “What part of your process feels most manageable?”
  • • “What resources have you used so far?”
  • • “What would help you feel like you have a clearer next step?”

Their responses should guide the type of process-focused support you provide.

“I don't even know where to start.”

→ Focus on planning, task management, or treating writing as a non-linear process.

“I have so many ideas that I don't know how to organize them.”

→ Practice brainstorming, outlining, or another pre-writing strategy.

“I don't understand what this requirement means.”

→ Help the student identify and write down the specific question they need to ask their Course Instructor.

“I don't understand the content well enough to answer this.”

→ Shift toward an appropriate study strategy or connect the student with their Course Instructor.

Students Do Not Have to Write in Order

Students sometimes assume they must complete a Performance Assessment from beginning to end in the exact order it appears.

If a student feels stuck, you can coach them in thinking about their writing process differently.

“Which part of the work feels most approachable right now?”

“What would help you build some momentum?”

“Would starting somewhere other than the beginning make this feel more manageable?”

The student determines where they begin and what they write.

Writing does not have to be a linear process.

Using the Rubric as an Organizational Tool

A rubric can help students see how a Performance Assessment is structured.

If an approved task template already exists within the course, encourage the student to use that resource first.

If one is not available, students may use the visible structure of the rubric to help organize their own document.

For example, rubric headings may help a student create sections so the task feels less overwhelming.

Keep the Rubric Boundary Clear

You may help a student recognize that the rubric can be used as an organizational structure.

You may not:

  • • Explain what a rubric item means
  • • Reword a rubric requirement for the student
  • • Tell the student what information belongs under a rubric item
  • • Review what the student wrote under a section
  • • Determine whether they addressed the requirement
  • • Tell them whether their response meets expectations

If the student asks:

“Did I answer this correctly?”
“Is this what they're asking for?”
“Do I have enough here?”
“Can you look at what I wrote?”

Those are evaluative questions and should be directed to the appropriate Course Instructor.

Organization is coaching. Interpretation and evaluation are not.

When Students Don't Understand a Task Prompt

Students may sometimes become stuck because they do not understand what a task or rubric requirement is asking.

Peer interns should not interpret the requirement for them. Instead, help the student recognize and articulate the uncertainty.

“What part of the prompt feels unclear to you?”

“What specifically would you want clarified before you continue?”

“How could you phrase that question for your Course Instructor?”

Students may also use approved WGU resources, such as resources explaining common directive terms used in Performance Assessments, to strengthen their general understanding of academic language.

However, those resources should not be used by the peer intern to determine or explain what a specific task requirement means.

Prepare the Student to Ask

Instead of

“I think this prompt wants you to...”

Try

“Let's identify exactly what feels unclear so you can take that question to your Course Instructor.”

An Appointment Planning document or similar tool can help students capture those questions.

Brainstorm the Process — Not the Student's Answer

Pre-writing strategies can help students organize their thoughts before drafting.

Brainstorming
Outlining
Mind mapping
Questioning
Graphic organizers

Peer interns can teach or demonstrate how the strategy works, but should not use the strategy to generate the student's PA content with them.

For example, you might demonstrate how someone could create an outline structure or brainstorming space using neutral material. Then ask the student to apply that process independently to their own work.

“What type of planning method usually helps you organize your ideas?”

“How could you structure a brainstorming space before you begin drafting?”

“What information or resources do you still need before you're ready to write?”

“What questions do you need answered before you can move forward?”

The student generates the ideas. The student writes the response. The peer intern supports the process.

Use Course Resources Before Creating New Solutions

Students may overlook resources already available within the course.

Task templates
Task guides
Task instruction videos
Course Instructor cohorts
Course tips or FAQs
Writing and citation resources

Peer interns can help students locate and access these resources.

Do more than simply point to a link. Ask students to reflect on how the resource could support their process.

“How could this resource fit into your plan for completing the task?”

“Which part of your current challenge might this help with?”

“When could you use this resource as you continue working?”

If the resource raises questions about the actual task requirements, those questions should go to the Course Instructor.

Research, Citations, and Writing Support

Peer interns can help students locate appropriate WGU resources, but they are not Librarians, Writing Coaches, Course Instructors, or Evaluators.

Peer Interns Can

✓

Show students where the WGU Library and writing resources are located

✓

Connect students with approved citation and paraphrasing resources

✓

Introduce general planning, organizing, and writing strategies

✓

Help students identify questions they want to ask a Librarian or Writing Coach

✓

Encourage students to use appropriate WGU support services

Peer Interns Do Not

×

Select sources for the student's task

×

Determine whether a source is appropriate for the task

×

Correct citations or reference pages

×

Proofread or edit the Performance Assessment

×

Rewrite student work

×

Review portions of the student's submission

×

Provide sentences, examples, ideas, or content for the student to submit

If a student needs specialized support, remain engaged by helping them identify what they need and connect with the appropriate resource.

Professional Communication and Self-Review

Students may also struggle with organization, clarity, grammar, punctuation, or other elements of professional communication. Peer interns should not proofread the student's Performance Assessment. Instead, coach the student in strategies they can use to review their own work.

Read Aloud

Students can read their own writing aloud to notice areas that sound unclear or awkward.

Review in Chunks

Rather than reviewing everything at once, students can focus on one element at a time:

Sentence clarity · Grammar · Spelling · Punctuation · Organization

Use a Time Delay

Students may benefit from taking a break from their writing before returning to review it with fresh eyes.

A peer intern can explain or demonstrate these strategies, but the student performs the review independently.

Do not ask the student to share their writing so you can identify errors for them.

Similarity and Academic Authenticity

Students may have questions or concerns about similarity in their Performance Assessments.

Peers can help students explore their writing process and connect them with approved WGU resources on academic authenticity, paraphrasing, citation, and similarity.

“How are you keeping track of your sources as you write?”

“What process are you using to put source information into your own words?”

“How are you distinguishing your ideas from information that came from a source?”

“What part of paraphrasing feels most challenging?”

Maintain the Boundary

Peer interns do not:

  • • Review the student's paper for plagiarism
  • • Review highlighted similarity matches
  • • Determine whether writing is sufficiently original
  • • Rewrite passages to reduce similarity
  • • Tell the student what wording to use
  • • Interpret academic integrity decisions

Help students strengthen their process and connect them with the appropriate WGU resource when additional support is needed.

When a Course Instructor Is Needed

Some PA questions belong with a Course Instructor.

What a task prompt means
What a rubric requirement is asking
What content should be included
Whether the student answered the question
Whether their response meets the rubric
Whether something is correct or sufficient
Evaluator feedback requiring academic clarification
Course-specific expectations

Redirecting does not mean ending the conversation. Before referring the student, help them clarify what they are confused about and formulate a useful question.

Instead of

“You'll need to ask your CI.”

Try

“Let's identify exactly what you're unsure about so you have a clear question to take to your Course Instructor.”

An Appointment Planning document can be used to capture those questions and help the student prepare for the conversation.

Remember

When coaching around Performance Assessments:

Do not review. Do not write. Do not interpret. Do not evaluate.

Help the student

Plan → Organize → Practice a Process → Reflect → Connect

Students should leave the session with something they can do next—a planning strategy, a clearer workflow, a resource located, or a specific question prepared for the appropriate WGU support person.

The Performance Assessment always remains the student's work.
