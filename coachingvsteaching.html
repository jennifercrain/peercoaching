Coaching, Teaching, Advising, Avoiding

Recognize the difference, stay within role boundaries, and keep students moving forward.

The Difference Is in the Action

Teaching gives answers. Advising gives suggestions. Coaching creates action.

Peer interns...

1

Help students become resourceful. Teaching actions are only used when sharing an example of applying a strategy—not course content.

2

Coach students on how they can change their approach to their course or how they will incorporate and apply resources.

3

Use personal experience with academic strategies and resources to relate and build trust when appropriate—not to advise, direct, or solve problems for students.

Four Approaches at a Glance

Use the goal, actions, common statements, and guidance below to recognize what is happening in an interaction.

Coaching

Creates action

Goal

Guide the student’s thinking to facilitate their independence—be the “guard rails.”

Actions

Questioning, Listening, Prompting, Reflecting, Guiding, Facilitating

Example Statements

“What have you tried?”

“What do you already know about this?”

“What feels most overwhelming?”

“When you read this, what stands out?”

When to Use

Any time, as often as possible. Question what students have tried, how they’re processing information, where they feel stuck, and what they will do next.

Teaching

Gives answers

Goal

Give your own knowledge and expertise to another person.

Actions

Explaining, Defining, Demonstrating, Showing, Telling, Modeling, Evaluating, Quizzing

Example Statements

“This means…”

“This is when...”

“The difference is...”

“This is wrong because...”

When to Use

Briefly during Focused Instruction (“I do”) when sharing how you use a strategy or locate a resource—not directing students to do exactly what you do.

Never focused on content. Period. No exception.

Advising

Gives suggestions

Goal

Direct the student’s next steps or actions with no practice or demonstration attached.

Actions

Recommending, Suggesting, Directing, Instructing, Telling, Dictating

Example Statements

“What you need to do is...”

“Start with…”

“I’d say you should...”

“You can just...”

When to Use

Advising should be extremely limited. Keep it focused on the student by following up with powerful questions that empower their own decision making.

Avoiding

Stops the process

Goal

Redirect or disengage without supporting the student’s learning process.

Actions

Dismissing, Deflecting, Redirecting Prematurely, Passing Off Responsibility

Example Statements

“That’s for your CI.”

“I can’t help with that.”

“You should try an Academic Coach.”

When to Use

Never. Peer interns follow the redirecting process to stay within support role boundaries before sending students elsewhere.

What Happens When Peers Shift Out of Coaching?

When Peers Teach

They can end up focusing too much on solving the immediate issue instead of helping the student build skills they can use across courses. This can lead to confusion if something is explained incorrectly or outside of scope, which can make things harder for the student later on during assessments.

When Peers Advise

They may share information that worked for them but is not necessarily best practice or applicable to all situations. Without sourcing information, guidance may be incorrect, outdated, or not apply across courses, situations, or schools at WGU. Advising without coaching and demonstrating also leaves no action attached for the student to practice.

When Peers Avoid

This can leave students stuck, slowing their progress and wasting valuable time that could have been used to move them forward. It also limits opportunities for meaningful peer-to-peer connection and shared problem-solving.

Scenario Examples

Compare how the same student request changes depending on whether the interaction uses coaching, teaching, advising, or avoiding.

Your student is looking for guidance on strategies to influence others.

“I keep failing the chapter quiz. On question 2, I don’t understand why my answer is wrong.”

Coaching

“Walk me through how you’re breaking down this question and what you think it’s asking.”

“Let’s try the process of elimination. Based on what you said, which do you think we can get rid of?”

“How would you check your understanding?”

The peer stays curious, asking about the student’s approach and thought process without taking over.

Teaching

“Okay, so on question 2 it’s asking you which statement is accurate when it comes to making personal appeals with credibility, logic, and emotion.”

“A, C, and D are all wrong because all three are effective; it just really depends on context and what you’re trying to do. Think about it—when would logic be bad in a presentation? The other answers don’t make sense.”

The peer jumps in, explains the question, and explains the answer, limiting the student’s opportunity to process independently.

Advising

“You should click through all the answer choices; it will tell you why it’s wrong.”

“You can also go into WGU Connect and see if there’s any resources there or make a post and ask.”

The peer tells the student what to do and where to go, versus demonstrating or practicing.

Avoiding

“Sorry, I can’t help you with the quizzes. Content is for your CI because they’re subject matter experts; I’m just a peer.”

The peer minimizes what they can offer and sends the student to another person.

Your student is looking for guidance on their performance assessment.

“I was hoping you could look over my task for me and let me know if it’s ready to submit. I’m worried I’m missing something.”

Coaching

“How do you usually check your work?”

“Let’s look at some PA resources together. This one here talks about organizing your paper using the A, A1, etc. headers. Using this, how might you change the way your task is laid out?”

“If we put these headers into a checklist, what feels incomplete? How might you expand that idea?”

The peer gives the student steps and a repeatable process they can use to check their work.

Teaching

“Absolutely, let’s look at it together.”

“So, in this section, it wants you to explain the difference between a learning objective and a learning goal and give some examples. You only explained the objective. Think of the objectives as the immediate next steps and the goal as the overall broad idea that the steps are moving you towards.”

“This sounds like you’re quoting something. You’ll want to insert a citation.”

The peer is evaluating the student’s work and providing instruction on what they did or need to do.

Advising

“What I always do is just submit it. The worst case is I got the PA back and submitted it again. We have unlimited attempts. That’s always worked for me; I recommend going ahead and submitting it to get that evaluator’s feedback.”

The peer gives misguided guidance based on personal experience that may not reflect best practices or support skill development.

Avoiding

“I can’t look at your task with you. That’s more of something that a writing coach in the Academic Coaching Center can help you with.”

The peer sends the student to another department without attempting to support them.

Practice: What Approach Is This?

Choose the approach that best describes each response. Feedback appears immediately after you select an answer.

1. A student says they do not know where to begin. The peer asks, “What feels most manageable right now, and what could your first step be?”

2. A peer says, “This is wrong because the concept actually means...” and then explains the course content.

3. A peer responds, “What you need to do is start with the template and then submit it once you finish the first section.”

4. A student asks for help getting unstuck. The peer immediately says, “That’s for your CI. I can’t help with that.”

5. A peer briefly demonstrates how they use a planning strategy, then asks the student how they might adapt the process for their own work.

Keep the student thinking. Keep the student practicing. Keep the student moving.
